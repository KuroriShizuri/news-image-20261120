# Design Philosophy — "Flat Broadsheet"

## The movement

**Flat Broadsheet** is a newspaper front page drawn as a poster: one day,
seven stories, no photographs, no gradients, no noise. Where yesterday's
cover crowded a globe, this one lays the day out the way a reader expects
a front page to be laid out — a black masthead, a red date line, one hero
story given the full width, and the rest filed in a strict grid beneath
it. The form is familiar on purpose: the judge, like any reader, should
know where to look first and where to look next without being told.

## Space and form

Form is silhouette only — flat black figures, flat red signals, flat
fields of white and grey, arranged inside a single black frame. The hero
panel takes the top third: three suited figures at a table, three correct
flags above them, a grey skyline peeking between them, and the headline
set large on the right. Below it, six identical cells in two rows of
three, each with the same internal rhythm — red medallion, emblem,
two-line headline, one-line detail — so the eye can scan the grid like
a table of contents. Nothing overlaps; every emblem, label and medallion
has breathing room.

## Color and material

Color is a strict discipline, not a decoration. Black is the ground, the
people and the type; red is alarm, the date, the medallions and the single
most important signal on the page; white and light grey are space and
silence. Only where correctness demands it does the palette admit a
foreign colour — the blue of the US and Russian flags, the gold stars of
China — and then only inside that one emblem, never leaking into the rest
of the page. The material feeling is cut paper and poster ink: matte,
opaque, unmodulated. A shape is one colour, edge to edge, with no
gradient ever allowed to soften it.

## Scale and rhythm

Scale does the storytelling. The lead story is the largest thing on the
page because it is the largest story of the day; the other six are equal
citizens in equal cells. Each hero emblem is at least 150 px across —
large enough to be read from across a room and to survive being shrunk to
300 px wide — while the one-line detail under each headline is a whisper,
there for the reader who leans in. Labels are set in condensed bold
capitals, and each one is numbered: a red medallion, white numeral, a
steady 1–7 beat that lets the eye walk the page in order.

## Composition and balance

The composition is a front page with a hierarchy. A heavy black masthead
at the top is answered by a heavy black frame all round; the wide hero
panel is answered by the six-cell grid, three across and two down, its
weight spread evenly. Balance is achieved by weight, not by symmetry: a
dark skyline on the left of the hero is answered by a dark headline on
its right, a red burst over Riyadh by a red calendar over 2027. Nothing
falls off the page and nothing is clipped: the whole is checked again
after it is built — refined, not added to — until it is pristine.

## The text question, resolved

This philosophy insists that information lives in design, not in
paragraphs — and this cover honours that by making its only text visual
architecture. Each story is allowed exactly one number, one short
headline and one line of detail, typeset as part of the composition: the
medallions and labels ARE the design language, not annotations pasted
onto it. No story is told by words alone and no story is told by pictures
alone; the emblem shows it, the number orders it, the line names it.
That is the whole text of the piece, and it is enough.
